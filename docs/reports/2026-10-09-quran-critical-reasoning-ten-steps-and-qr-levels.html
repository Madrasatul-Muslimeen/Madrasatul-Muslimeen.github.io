Ten Steps and the QR Levels

How the ten steps of “Quran for Critical Reasoning: the process of contemplating on the Quran” line up with the Approaches, the QCR collections and the year levels in QR; where the intention belongs; and whether the ten steps are enough for an unbiased understanding of the Qur'an, Hadith and Deen.

Sources read: the article (full text); the 30 Approaches in the app's code, with your one-line glosses from the original app; QCR (18 collections, 379 āyāt, from your file QCR — Qur'an (calls for) Critical Reasoning v01.13); the Qur'an grammar data QR already carries. Not read: the 10 Approaches you added yourself. They live only in your own data, which this workspace cannot open (question 1).

1. Your views (9 Oct 2026)

In your words, lightly edited for English only. They now set the frame for the rest of this file.

The intention is a mindset, not a level

The intention is not a level. It is a mindset: the approach a person must bring to the Qur'an as a whole. It does not count for any single unit, so it is never recorded or claimed. But a person needs to be reminded of it often.

Why the scholars come last

The understanding of the scholars is deliberately not placed at the beginning, because it influences people. It is there only for reconciliation: to check whether the student's own understanding has a basis, that it is not wild, and that it is free of bias, prejudice and outside influence. That reconciliation is set out in more detail in the Approaches of the section “Understanding of the Scholars”, which order the scholars by their time and level.

The aim is that one day the student can stand on their own Fahm (understanding), and their own Fatwa (ruling), even when nobody else has reached it.

Corrections this makes to the first version of this file:

• The intention is no longer listed as a level. It is a standing mindset, and section 2 answers where the app should remind of it.

• Your reason for placing the scholars last answers the earlier question on order. Reconciling with the scholars must come before standing on one's own Fahm and Ḥukm. So, in QR, “Understanding of the Scholars” would sit before section 6 (Judgement / Authority: 24 Fiqh to ruling, 25 Fahm, 26 Ḥukm), not after it. Question 2 now asks you only to confirm this.

2. The intention: where it fits best

Because the intention belongs to the Qur'an as a whole, its place is wherever study begins, not on any unit, wheel slice or record. Four places in the app meet a reader at that moment. My recommendation is the first two together.

1

When study opens, once a day recommended

The first time in a day a person opens the Qur'an to read or study, one line sits above the āyāt: one of the four intentions, in turn. For example: “This Qur'an was revealed to me, for me. What is its instruction for me today?” One tap on Bismillah closes it for the day. It is short enough to read every day without growing stale, because it rotates. It also applies to Hadith and Dua, since the article's third intention is “read an Āyah or a Hadith”, so it would be one reminder for the whole of MMSA rather than a Qur'an-only one. How often (every time, once a day, or off) can be a setting for each person.

2

In the Note, at the moment of contemplating recommended

When a Note is opened under a Critical Reasoning Approach (14–26), the empty Note shows a faint prompt before the first word is typed: “It is for me. What is its instruction for me?” This is where the article's third intention turns into work. It disappears as soon as the person writes.

3

The tagline strip nothing to build

The moving line under the app's name (today “Study ONE Hadith at Bed (before sleep)” and the others) is already yours to edit in the Taglines page. The four intentions can join it today, with no build at all.

4

As a dua before study

The fourth intention is itself a dua. It can sit at the top of the Dua tab as the dua to say before studying, and it belongs with Approach 16 (Deriving Dua).

What it should never be: a slice of the wheel, a “Not started / Achieved” status, or a count. A mindset that is scored becomes a box to tick.

3. Do the ten steps cover everything?

For contemplating the meaning of an āyah by a common person, they cover the core very well. They move from what the text says, to what it asks of me, to its inner and rhetorical meaning, to patterns across the Qur'an, and only then to other people and the scholars. That order protects the reader's own reasoning, as you intend.

For an unbiased understanding of the Qur'an, Hadith and the Deen as a whole, and above all for a Fatwa, six things are missing or only implied. Each is a principle widely recognised in the study of the Qur'an and Hadith. Each also guards against bias rather than adding someone else's opinion.

A

Context: the passage around the āyah, and why it came down

An āyah read alone can be bent to almost any meaning. The steps never say “read the passage before and after it, and the whole Sūrah's theme”, nor “know the occasion of its revelation (asbāb al-nuzūl)”. Cherry-picking, taking one āyah out of its passage, is the most common way bias enters.

Where it fits: the Primary level, beside step 2 (subject). QR already shows the Rukūʿ and the Sūrah around every āyah.

B

The Qur'an explaining the Qur'an

Step 7 looks for patterns. It never says the plainer rule: before concluding, gather every āyah on the same subject, because one āyah often explains, limits or completes another. A conclusion drawn from one āyah when ten speak to the subject is a biased conclusion, even when every step was followed.

Where it fits: the Advanced level, as step 7's first task. QR's Related āyāt, QCR collections and word search are the tools.

C

The Sunnah: the explanation that came with the revelation

The article names Hadith in the intention (“read an Āyah or a Hadith”) but no step turns to it. Yet the Prophet ﷺ explained and lived the Qur'an: the Qur'an commands ṣalāh, and the Sunnah shows how. This is not the scholars' influence that your view guards against. It is revelation explaining revelation, and it comes from the same source as the āyah.

A correction I'd suggest to the order: keep the scholars' opinions last, as you say, but place the authentic Sunnah on the subject before them, at the Advanced level beside step 7.

D

For Hadith: is it authentic?

The steps assume a sound text, which the Qur'an always is. A hadith is not. Before contemplating a hadith, the first question is its grade (ṣaḥīḥ, ḥasan, ḍaʿīf…). Reasoning carefully over a weak or fabricated narration only gives a careful wrong answer.

Where it fits: a step 0 for Hadith. The HadeethEnc cards already show the grade.

E

A check on one's own bias

Your aim is understanding free of bias, prejudice and influence. Bias also comes from inside, from what a person wishes the āyah said. Step 9 (discussion without arrogance) touches this. One sharper question at the end of step 8 would make it a habit: “What would show that my understanding is wrong? Am I reading what it says, or what I want it to say?” Two more: separate what is certain from what is only probable (step 4 already speaks of certain knowledge), and end with “Allah knows best”.

F

Before a Fatwa: the tools of ruling

Your goal is that a student can one day stand on their own Fahm and Fatwa. The Qur'an itself values that (“so that they may gain understanding in the Deen”, 9:122, the pivot of your Approach 24). Here the two words need to be kept apart:

• Fahm, understanding for one's own life, is open to every sincere reader who follows the steps.

• Fatwa, a ruling that others act on, needs more than the ten steps. It needs knowing which āyāt or hadith abrogate or are abrogated, general against specific, absolute against restricted, consensus, and the objectives of the Sharīʿah. Without these, a sincere person can reach a ruling the texts themselves do not support.

Your section 6 (24 Fiqh to ruling, 25 Fahm, 26 Ḥukm) is the place for these tools. Approach 24's own guide already says “under guidance”. The 9 Approaches of “Understanding of the Scholars”, ordered by time and level, are where they would be learned. I'd suggest naming them explicitly there, so that standing on one's own Fatwa is earned by these tools and does not stand against them.

In short: the ten steps are a sound method of contemplation. For an unbiased understanding they need context (A), the Qur'an on the whole subject (B) and the Sunnah (C) before the scholars, a grade check for hadith (D), and a habit of questioning oneself (E). For a Fatwa they need the tools of ruling (F). None of these brings in the scholars early. A, B, C and D come from the texts themselves, and E from the reader.

4. The article, step by step

The author's point: contemplating the Qur'an is ordinary common sense that people “keep locked” for the Qur'an. They call it Critical Reasoning so a common person is not afraid of it. Tafsir comes last, “to reconcile your understanding”. The first eight steps are done on your own. The method is meant for the family, with children over 7: “print it and stick it on the wall of their study table”.

7. Where they disagree

1. The order of the scholars and rulings: answered by your view, to confirm. QR puts Judgement / Authority (24–26) before Discussion (27), and in your data “Understanding of the Scholars” comes after Judgement / Authority. Your view says reconciling with the scholars comes before standing on one's own Fahm or Fatwa. So the order would become: own reasoning (14–23), then Discussion (27), then Understanding of the Scholars, then Judgement / Authority (24–26). This needs your yes (question 2): it moves sections, it adds nothing.
2. Year bands against the level names. In QCR, 21 Tadabbur, “Advanced” is Y5, while 20 Tafakkur, “Intermediate” is Y6–12. In the article, Intermediate comes before Advanced. Either Tadabbur's band is low because its four āyāt are short and clear (4:82, 23:68, 38:29, 47:24 all ask “do they not ponder?”), or one band is a slip (question 4).
3. The article's first step has no home in QR. “What type of āyah is it?” is the first question asked of every āyah, and there is nowhere to mark an āyah as a command, a prohibition, a warning or a description. Today a reader can only write the answer in a Note.

What the grammar data can already suggest for step 1

QR already carries the Quranic Arabic Corpus grammar for every word. Counted across all 6,236 āyāt:

1,309āyāt with a command (imperative verb, or the lām of command)
289with a prohibition (lā of forbidding)
830with a question
866with a condition (“if…”)
356with a call (“O you who…”)
4,795with neither command nor prohibition

These are suggestions, never answers. A command spoken by Firʿawn or by the disbelievers, quoted in an āyah, is not a command to the reader. Telling them apart is exactly the reasoning step 1 trains, so the computer can only point and a person decides. Warning, recommended and not-recommended have no grammar mark at all.
